Estimated daily use

Back to About

The percentage beside a dictionary entry estimates the chance of seeing or hearing that word at least once in a day. It is derived from the word's frequency rank using a simple Zipf model, not from tracking anyone's personal language use.

Calculation

Let r be the word's frequency rank, where rank 1 is most frequent. The model approximates its probability per token as p = 1 / (r × H₅₀₀₀₀), where H₅₀₀₀₀ ≈ ln(50,000) + 0.5772156649 is the harmonic normalizer.

Assuming T = 17,000 spoken and written tokens per day, the expected daily occurrence rate is λ = p × T. The chance of one or more occurrences is estimated as 100 × (1 − exp(−λ)), then rounded to a whole percent.

The same numbers can be read three ways: p × 1,000,000 gives occurrences per million words, λ gives the expected number of uses per day, and 1 − exp(−λ) gives the probability of at least one use.

Example

For rank 802, the model estimates a daily-use probability of about 84%. This means the model predicts at least one occurrence on roughly 84 out of 100 comparable days; it is not a guarantee that an individual will encounter the word on any particular day.

Limitations

This is a rough estimate. It assumes a Zipf exponent of 1, a 50,000-word cutoff, and the same frequency distribution across spoken and written language. A person's vocabulary, interests, location, and daily context can make their actual exposure quite different. Real usage is also bursty rather than evenly spread, so the percentages should be read as an order-of-magnitude guide, not a precise forecast.
